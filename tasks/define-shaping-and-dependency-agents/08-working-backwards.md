# Working backwards: two launch-day narratives and the hard questions

Date: 2026-10-31. Part of the definition of two enterprise product capabilities ([00 overview](00-overview.md)). This document uses the press-release-and-FAQ form ([an insider look at the working-backwards process](../../research/sources/aboutamazon-insider-look-working-backwards.md), first-party): write the launch announcement first, then the questions a sceptical reader would ask, and let both discipline what gets built. Two announcements follow, one for feature teams and one for platform, process and gate owners, then an internal FAQ of the hard questions. Both are set at the **first serious deployment**: a walk-stage agent in a verified environment ([07, Stage 2](07-staged-roadmap.md)), not the destination. Every claim is one a pilot could check; where a number appears it is marked illustrative and the measure that would replace it is named. The organisation is a fictional, unnamed retail bank; people are roles.

---

## Announcement 1: for feature teams

### Every feature now starts with what must be true and who has to act, on day one

*Two shared, persistent modules, intent shaping and delivery dependency mapping, turn a feature goal into validated requirements and a delivery map with owners, so teams find in week one what they used to find in week nine.*

The bank's product organisation today opened intent shaping and delivery dependency mapping, running in one shared session per feature, to every team shipping features on the servicing app and the lending journeys. Product managers, business analysts, designers and engineers can bring a feature goal in whatever form they have it (a one-pager, a ticket, a prototype link, a voice note) and get back two things within the day: the requirements the feature carries here, including the enterprise obligations (law and regulation; enterprise policy and standards; platform and architecture standards; the business; contracts and counterparties), each with its source and the person who validates it; and a map of which platforms, processes and gates can satisfy each requirement, what onboarding to each takes, where real build work is needed, and what the mapping module could not find.

**The problem.** A feature that is small to describe touches a lot. A loan offer on the home screen needs a placement slot, an eligibility call, customer data under terms, a registered client, a promotion sign-off, a privacy screen, a change record, capacity on another team's plan, a release train and an answer to one question only the compliance reviewer can give. Today those are discovered in sequence, late, by asking around: three intake routes in three shapes, a review queue of unknown length, and a wording problem surfacing when a reviewer happens to see a mock-up. In the bank's last ten offer features, the median gap between a goal being agreed and the requirements being complete enough to plan was measured from ticket and document history; it is the baseline the modules are held to (the number is the pilot's to publish, not this announcement's).

**The solution.** The two modules work in one session that stays open for the life of the feature. **Intent shaping** reads past features of the same kind, the policy library and the published criteria of the gates, screens the goal against the bank's requirements categories, asks the person only what it could not read, and drafts the augmented requirements with a source on every line. A completeness check runs before anything is called ready, and it fails closed. **Delivery dependency mapping** takes each validated requirement and finds what satisfies it: the offers and decisioning engine for cohort targeting, the app's content system for the placement, the data product for eligibility data, and the gates that apply by their published criteria. Where two platforms could satisfy a requirement, it lays out the considerations it can find and asks the person to choose; the choice and the reasons are recorded for the next team. It draws the map by phase with evidence on every edge and a "could not find" list, drafts each ask as the smallest question the owner has to answer, posts it as itself on the person's behalf through the channel that owner reads, and tracks the answer. Every answer is written back with the owner's role, the date and the source, so the next team does not ask again.

**What a feature team sees.** On day one: the requirements, the map, the gap list and the drafted asks. Over the following days: owners answering in the session, from wherever they are; the stale runbook line struck through with the release note that supersedes it; a demand drafted for the decisioning team's capacity, ready for the sponsor's name; the sequence against the app's release train and the year-end freeze, with every date sourced or marked assumed. When the decisioning release slips, the plan moves, and only the owners affected hear, once. At any point: a one-paragraph status that says what is waiting and on whom.

**What the modules will not do.** They will not decide between platforms, approve at any gate, mark intent ready, invent a dependency or an obligation without a source, chase anyone outside the agreed rules, or report on people. The person steers; owners decide; the modules propose, submit where the platform's policy allows it, and record.

*"I brought a goal and a sketch. By the afternoon I had a list of what the bank requires of any offer, with the policy next to each line, and a map of the eight teams I needed and the one question I could not answer myself. The question went to the compliance reviewer with the two wordings attached. The answer came back the next morning and it is now on record with their name on it. The next team to put an offer on the home screen will not have to ask."* (The marketing product manager; fictional.)

**How to start.** Open a feature session from the product channel with the goal in any form. Intent shaping will say what it read, what it could not find, and what it needs from you. Reviewers and owners join when an ask reaches them; they need nothing but the link.

### FAQ for feature teams

**Is this a PRD generator?** No. Intent shaping produces a short, numbered, evidence-tied set of requirements and a map. If your team keeps a narrative document, the record links to it.

**Will it slow me down with questions?** Intent shaping asks only what it could not read, one at a time, with its own guess attached. The pilot measures how many questions each feature needed and how many changed an answer.

**Can I overrule it?** Everything it adds is provisional and visibly so. You can strike an obligation; the module records that you did and why, and the validator for that obligation sees it.

**What if the platform I need has no card?** Then the map says so, the gap goes on the "could not find" list, and the ask goes to the inferred owner marked as inferred. That gap, aggregated across features, is what gets the card written.

**What happens to my session when I go on leave?** It stays open under the modules' own identity, with you named as the person they act for. A colleague can be named to steer it. Nothing is submitted while nobody is steering unless a policy says it may be.

**Does engineering see the same thing?** Yes. The record is one place, readable by people and by the delivery agents, with the same evidence links.

## Announcement 2: for platform, process and gate owners

### Asks now arrive in one shape, your answers are kept, and your lead times are yours

*Delivery dependency mapping gives every platform, process and gate owner a demand signal, questions that can be answered from a phone, each question asked once, and the measured lead time nobody had.*

The bank's platform engineering group today confirmed that delivery dependency mapping, the module feature teams use to find what their features need from others, is now the way most asks reach the app platform, the offers and decisioning engine, the data products on the common paths, and the change, privacy and promotion gates. Owners of those platforms and gates receive asks in a single shape, see their own demand and lead times, and verify the module's description of their platform once rather than explaining it to each team.

**The problem.** A platform team's week is spent answering the same questions in different shapes: a form from one team, a demand with a business case from another, a chat message from a third who knows someone. No platform at the bank publishes how long its intake takes, because nobody measures it; the teams that depend on the platform guess, and the platform team cannot make its case for capacity. Published criteria go unread; reviewers answer by email what a page already says. And the records that describe the platform are stale: the catalogue names an owner who moved teams, and the runbook says batch where the release note says real time.

**The solution.** The module reads what the platform already publishes, drafts a card in a standard shape (purpose, owner, contract, intake, checks, approvers, lead time, calendar), and asks the owner to verify it only when a feature actually needs it, with the feature's question attached. Each ask is the smallest question the owner must answer, with the requirement it serves, the evidence and what happens if the answer is no; owners answer from wherever they are, and the answer is recorded once with their role, the date and the source. The module times every ask from its own timestamps, so each platform and gate gets its lead time measured from real requests, per platform and never per person. Across features it reports the demand signal: which features depend on the platform, what they ask for most, and what they could not find.

**What an owner sees.** A card for their platform to verify or correct, with an expiry so that it is never silently stale. Asks in one shape, grouped so nobody receives five messages. A monthly view of their own demand and lead time. When a team's agent finds the runbook is wrong, a drafted correction to accept, fixed at its home and not only in the module's record. For the gates: an applicability pre-screen from the gate's own published criteria, and for data gates with published evidence lists, packets that have passed the deterministic checks before a reviewer opens them. The module never approves; the reviewer does, and the decision is recorded.

**What it asks of owners.** Nothing up front. Three to five anchor platforms and gates were curated with their owners before the pilot by agreement. Everyone else meets the module when an ask arrives. An owner who prefers asks in this shape can publish an intake description in it; an owner whose intake is already a schema can let the module submit to it, with the owner's own policy deciding what is routine.

*"The first ask I got had the requirement, both documents that disagreed and a one-line question. I answered it from my phone. The second week, a card for our engine arrived for me to check; two lines were wrong and I fixed them. We now have a number for how long our intake takes, from real requests, which we never had. I used it in the capacity conversation."* (The offers and decisioning owner; fictional.)

**How to start.** Owners are not asked to start anything. Owners who want their platform's card and intake description in place before the first ask arrives can write them in the standard shape; the agent-ready checklist says what each item is ([06 MVP, section 4](../research-dependency-agent/06-mvp.md)).

### FAQ for owners

**Will this flood us with requests?** The module does not hold requests and does not chase outside the agreed rules (suppress when work is moving, name the blocker, cap the frequency). It asks nothing the feature does not need. The pilot publishes the ask volume per platform and the response rate; if either goes the wrong way, the rules tighten.

**Who sees our lead times?** The platform's lead time is the platform's. The pilot's default is that each owner sees their own; how the view across platforms is shared is a decision for platform leadership, laid out as options in [07, section 3](07-staged-roadmap.md). Nothing is reported per person.

**Does the module change our intake?** No. It drafts into your route and tracks there. The standard shape is its output format; it becomes your input format only if you prefer asks that way.

**Can it approve on our behalf?** No, and not at the destination either for judgement gates. For data gates with a published policy for what is routine, the policy decides, the decision is recorded automatically, and your reviewers see the exceptions.

**What if the card it drafts is wrong?** It is provisional and says so until you verify it. Correct it or reject it; the module records which. Unverified cards expire and the map shows them as unverified.

**What does the second line get?** Evidence packets assembled from the spec, the decisions and the map, checked against the gate's published list before submission; a record per feature of what was asked, answered and decided; and, across features, which gates never say no, which is the beginning of a conversation about what each gate is for.

## Internal FAQ: the hard questions

Written for the sponsors, the second line and the people who will be asked to fund and run this. Each answer says what is known, what is a design choice and what the pilot would have to show.

**Whose identity do the modules use, and who is accountable when one posts an ask or submits an intake?**
At the walk stage: an agent identity of their own (one, or one per module, depending on the packaging in [00](00-overview.md)), with a named sponsor and a succession rule, acting on behalf of a named person, with both recorded on every request. The identity model is the decision underneath every interaction a shared agent has ([shared-agents synthesis, A6](../research-shared-persistent-agents/01-synthesis.md)), and every shipped answer has a documented leakage mode: running on the requester's token, on the creator's token, or as a service per channel. Identity providers now treat an agent as a principal with sponsors ([Entra agent sponsors](../../research/sources/microsoft-entra-agent-owners-sponsors.md), first-party), and the standards for delegation exist ([RFC 8693](../../research/sources/ietf-rfc8693-oauth-token-exchange.md)). What the pilot must settle with identity and security owners before Stage 2: which model, what scope per session, who can revoke, and whether platform and gate owners accept "requested by the agent on behalf of" as a record. At Stage 1 the question does not arise, because a person sends everything.

**Why should anyone trust what the modules write back?**
Nobody should, until a named person has verified it, and the record shows which entries are verified and which are provisional. The design does not choose one trust mechanism; it defines what trusted context has to achieve (trustworthiness, authoritativeness, freshness, provenance, coverage, consistency, contestability, cost to the owner, fitness to drive rules) and measures candidate mechanisms against those criteria ([04](04-context-trust-criteria.md)). Three facts shape the defaults: records written by hand drift (half of engineers do not trust their system of record, [Port survey](../../research/sources/port-state-of-idp-2025-metadata-trust.md), vendor survey); records written at decision time stay fresh ([04 destination, legibility ladder](../research-dependency-agent/04-destination.md)); and the documentation complaint at one company fell steeply only after an owner-verify-or-archive rule ([Stripe panel](../../research/sources/stripe-sessions-2024-10x-engineer-panel.md), first-party). So: provisional by default, owner verification riding on real asks, expiry, and the verification and staleness rates reported from week one. If owners do not verify, the record does not improve, and the pilot will say so.

**Won't this become a bureaucrat: a module that fills every form and satisfies every gate so well that nobody asks whether the gate should exist?**
It is the main risk at the destination and the research names it ([04 destination, section 5](../research-dependency-agent/04-destination.md)). Three answers. The design rule is that the modules make a gate easier to satisfy and never easier to skip, because the sanction for a skipped gate is real ([Finansinspektionen](../../research/sources/fi-swedbank-change-management-sanction.md), regulator), but the same module reports which gates never say no and which obligations never change a design, and that report is the material for retiring them. A change board that approved over 90% of major changes ([FCA](../../research/sources/fca-implementing-technology-change.md), regulator) is a board that could be asked what it is for. Second, approvals stay with people until a gate's owner writes a policy for what is routine, and the policy's class has a person on every change. Third, both modules are first line only at judgement gates by construction. The honest caveat: the counterweight only works if someone reads the signal, and the options for who does are open ([07, section 3](07-staged-roadmap.md)).

**What does it cost, and what is the cost of the status quo?**
The cost side of the status quo is independently evidenced: work spanning parties takes about two and a half times as long ([Herbsleb and Mockus](../../research/sources/herbsleb-mockus-speed-communication-distributed-development.md), independent); half of engineers at one large company chase the teams they depend on for status ([Microsoft Research](../../research/sources/microsoft-research-coordination-large-scale-development.md), independent); one IT project in six overruns by about 200% ([Flyvbjerg and Budzier](../../research/sources/flyvbjerg-budzier-it-project-riskier.md), independent). The benefit side is first-party or vendor and has no control group, so the case leads with the cost of the status quo and treats benefit figures as illustrations ([03 value](../research-dependency-agent/03-value.md)). The running cost of the modules: no per-session cost for any shared agent system is published; the one gateway data point for a coding harness puts spend at a long-tailed mean per active user per month with a much lower median ([shared-agents synthesis, E](../research-shared-persistent-agents/01-synthesis.md)); the pilot meters per session and per feature from day one and reports it beside time to ready. The larger cost is owners' attention, which is the scarce resource; the design spends it only on the smallest question riding a real ask, and the pilot reports the ask volume per owner kind.

**Why now?**
Four things changed at once. Agent identity became a first-class principal at every major identity provider ([04 destination, section 1.5](../research-dependency-agent/04-destination.md)), so "on behalf of" is now a thing a platform can record. Intake as a schema is the most machine-readable rung of platform legibility already, built by platform tooling over years ([Backstage templates](../../research/sources/backstage-docs-writing-templates.md), first-party). The regulator now requires the dependency map as a record and firms cannot yet produce it cleanly ([ESAs dry run](../../research/sources/esas-dora-dry-run-register-of-information.md), regulator), and asks for discovery before change design ([FCA](../../research/sources/fca-implementing-technology-change.md), regulator). And the delivery side is automating faster than the shaping side: coding agents merge a large share of changes at the companies the project studied (first-party and vendor claims, [Intent Factory](../../research/sources/geoff-building-accelerating-intent-factory.md)), so refinement is the bottleneck, and vague intent now reaches a coding agent faster than it used to reach an engineer. What has not changed: no shipped product discovers dependencies nobody declared ([04 destination, section 1.1](../research-dependency-agent/04-destination.md)), so this is not a buy decision yet.

**What if owners simply do not answer?**
Then the record does not improve, the anchors are the whole environment for longer, and the pilot's first quarter says so in the response rate per channel and the verification rate. The evidence on what gets ignored is clear: untargeted bot volume ([Dependabot](../../research/sources/arxiv-he-dependabot-exploratory-study.md), independent); what gets answered: a targeted reminder that names the blocker and is suppressed when work is moving, which cut overdue resolution time by 60% in a randomised trial ([Nudge](../../research/sources/arxiv-maddila-nudge-overdue-pull-requests.md), first-party). The design copies the second. If it still fails, the first environment investment goes to the two slowest, most-depended-on platforms by the module's own timings, by agreement with their owners, and mapping's value to feature teams is the map and the gap list alone, which is still more than today.

**Will making every ask and every wait visible chill people?**
It can. Broad visibility bred hiding and a performance for observers in one field experiment, and privacy zones raised output ([Bernstein](../../research/sources/asq-bernstein-transparency-paradox.md), independent); a public-by-default agent company reports that some questions "just don't get asked" ([shared-agents synthesis, E](../research-shared-persistent-agents/01-synthesis.md)). The defaults: the session's visibility is decided before launch and can be made private deliberately; waits are reported per platform and gate, never per person; the view across platforms is an option for platform leadership, not a default. The pilot asks owners and feature teams about this directly each quarter.

**How is this different from the planning tools the bank already owns?**
Every shipped planning agent reads the dependencies someone already recorded, checks that dates are consistent, and stops at the edge of its own instance ([04 destination, section 1.1](../research-dependency-agent/04-destination.md), a table of eight products). None takes a requirement and finds the platforms, gates and owners nobody declared; none routes an ask to an owner across platforms; none writes what it learns back where the next team reads it; none measures a platform's lead time. Mapping drafts into those tools (the demand into the portfolio tool, the change into the change tool) and tracks there; it does not replace them. Worth re-checking each quarter: a vendor shipping cross-platform discovery with sourced edges and a write-back store would change the build-or-buy answer.

**What if the model is wrong?**
It will be, at a measured rate. Language models over-predict dependencies from specifications without retrieved examples ([LEREDD](../../research/sources/arxiv-darif-leredd-requirement-dependencies.md), independent); inferred ownership is good enough to suggest, not to act on ([DeepTriage](../../research/sources/arxiv-pham-deeptriage-incident-routing.md), first-party). So: no edge or obligation without a source; owners proposed as inferred until confirmed; a weekly labelled sample graded by the people who know, with false positives and false negatives reported per category and per class; each right (posting, chasing, submitting) earned on that sample and revocable; a fresh-context judge on the spec and a person on a sample of the judge. The evaluation design is in [05, section 8](05-concept-intent-shaping.md) and [06, section 9](06-concept-delivery-dependency-mapping.md).

**Who owns it, and what happens if the owner leaves?**
A deployed shared agent needs a human steward, a sponsor for its identity and a succession rule; an agent nobody can say the purpose of should be retired ([shared-agents synthesis, C9](../research-shared-persistent-agents/01-synthesis.md)). The product organisation owns the modules; platform engineering owns the card store; the second line owns the gate policies and the ratification class; the data product owner owns the index. The intent records and the context layer live in stores the bank already trusts and stay readable without the modules, so if they are switched off, the record remains.

**What does the pilot have to show, and what is the exit if it does not?**
Four things ([05, section 7](05-concept-intent-shaping.md); [06, section 8](06-concept-delivery-dependency-mapping.md)): time to ready falls against the baseline without the late-surprise rate rising; late surprises and rework fall against a prospective control; context compounds, as a rising reuse rate and a verification rate owners sustain; and owners pull it in unmandated, as cards and intake descriptions written in the shape without being asked. If the first two hold and the last two do not, the modules are a single-player tool and are funded as one. If owners answer and verify but time to ready does not move, the bottleneck is elsewhere (the research's warning that priority churn outranks dependencies as a cause of slip, [DORA 2024](../../research/sources/dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-2024.md), independent), and the ask register's "waiting on a platform whose priorities changed" state will show it. If none holds, the record and the measured lead times remain, which is more than the bank had.

**Does this depend on choosing one agent, two, or skills in the harness we already run?**
No. The narratives above hold under any of the packagings in [00](00-overview.md), with one exception: posting, chasing and submitting as an agent need an identity that a skill running as the user does not have. The first comparison the project proposes, intent shaping as a task agent against a skill in a general harness with a human-scored rubric, is the way to find out whether that identity is worth the service it needs.
