# Concept brief: intent shaping

Date: 2026-10-31. One of two concept briefs (the other is [06 delivery dependency mapping](06-concept-delivery-dependency-mapping.md)); the map of the set is [00](00-overview.md). This brief describes the module as a thing worth building for a large, matrixed, platform-centric, regulated organisation. It builds on the project's two position papers on the intent factory and on intent shaping in a well-managed environment ([Intent Factory](../../research/sources/geoff-building-accelerating-intent-factory.md), [Intent Shaping](../../research/sources/geoff-intent-shaping-well-managed-environment.md), both first-party positions), the skill-spectrum profile of a shaping skill ([synthesis, section G](../research-skill-rigidity-spectrum/01-synthesis.md#g-worked-example-an-intent-shaping-skill-on-each-dimension)), and the S05 story ([stories](../user-story-mapping/stories-jobs.md#s05-write-and-ratify-a-spec-engineers-can-act-on), [worked derivation](../user-story-mapping/worked-derivation-s05.md)). The method it runs is [01](01-requirements-taxonomy.md) and [02](02-structured-inquiry.md); what it reads and writes is defined in [03](03-context-layer-model.md). Evidence is labelled first-party, independent, vendor claim or anecdote; examples are fictional and say so.

## 1. The problem, and who has it

A feature in this kind of organisation carries two sets of requirements. The first is the one the person brings: the problem, the treatment in mind, the behaviour they want. The second is the one the organisation imposes on any feature, whoever brings it: what regulation requires, what policy and standards require, what security, privacy, accessibility, brand, records and the platforms require. Throughout this brief, **requirement** covers both sets, and **enterprise obligation** is the label for a requirement whose source is outside the feature's own goal (law and regulation; enterprise policy and standards; platform and architecture standards; the business; contracts and counterparties: classes S2 to S6 in [01](01-requirements-taxonomy.md)). Today the first set is written down early and the second is discovered late, by a reviewer who happens to see a mock-up, by a gate in week nine, or by an engineer mid-delivery.

The cost is independently evidenced on the dependency side ([02 challenges](../research-dependency-agent/02-challenges.md)): the UK regulator's review of over a million changes found the most consistent failure factor was a project dependent on other projects, and asks firms to invest in discovery before designing change ([FCA](../../research/sources/fca-implementing-technology-change.md), regulator). On the shaping side the evidence is thinner and mostly first-party or anecdotal: refinement is the stated bottleneck, done bespoke each time, with context scattered across private documents and the people who know, and only a sprint or two of ready intent ahead of delivery ([Intent Factory, current state](../../research/sources/geoff-building-accelerating-intent-factory.md), first-party observation); PRD drafting is the most common PM use of AI and the generic PRD traces to missing context, not the model ([stories, S05 pain](../user-story-mapping/stories-jobs.md#s05-write-and-ratify-a-spec-engineers-can-act-on), vendor survey and anecdote).

Who has it: anyone turning a goal into something delivery can act on. A product manager writing a spec; a business analyst refining a change request; a designer whose prototype is about to be treated as the spec; a software engineer picking up a ticket that says what but not what else must be true. The project's working definition makes the module job-family agnostic for that reason: the task is the same whoever performs it.

## 2. The job

Take an existing feature goal and augment it with the enterprise obligations any feature carries here, then validate the result. In one line: **from "what I want" to "what must be true for this to ship here", with a source on every line.**

Three things it is not. It is not a PRD generator: it produces a short, evidence-tied set of requirements, not a narrative ([spec-standard options](../../options/DIMENSIONS.md#sub-choice-spec-standard--what-counts-as-agent-ready-intent)). It is not the risk assessor: it is first line, classifying which obligations apply and preparing them for the people who decide ([Intent Shaping](../../research/sources/geoff-intent-shaping-well-managed-environment.md), first-party position: risk review as a specification and classification problem). And it does not replace the person's goal: it augments it and challenges it, and the person remains the author.

## 3. Behaviour, step by step

The steps are fixed in order (read, screen, inquire, draft, check, route, record); what happens inside each is the model's. This is the profile the skill-spectrum guide recommends for shaping: rigid at gathering and classification, loose at interrogation, with the decision kept by a person ([design guide](../research-skill-rigidity-spectrum/03-design-guide.md)).

### Day one: a crawl agent in a scattered environment

1. **Intake.** The person states the goal, the treatment and the requirements they already have, in whatever form exists: a document, a ticket, a prototype link, a voice note. The module opens the feature's intent record (the convention in [03](03-context-layer-model.md)) and restates the goal in one paragraph for the person to correct.
2. **Read before asking.** It searches for past features of the same kind (enterprise search over specs, decks and wikis; the ticket tool; past intent records) and copies what obligations those carried and who validated them. It reads the policy library, the standards wikis and the published criteria of the anchor gates. It lists what it read and what it could not find. Nothing is asked of a person yet. (The rule, from the design guide: never ask what you can read.)
3. **Screen against the requirements categories.** (This is stage 0 and the start of stage 1 in [02](02-structured-inquiry.md).) For each category in [01](01-requirements-taxonomy.md) it asks the context, not the person, whether an enterprise obligation applies, by what heuristic, with what source; the screening questions are in [02](02-structured-inquiry.md). Each finding has a confidence and a validator (the named role who owns that obligation). Low confidence becomes a question.
4. **Inquire.** It puts the remaining questions to the person, one at a time, each with a guess and the reason it matters, grouped by category. It challenges any requirement that has no evidence behind it and says so (the stance Ramp's shaping skill takes, [Intent Factory](../../research/sources/geoff-building-accelerating-intent-factory.md), vendor claim relayed). The person's answers are recorded with their name.
5. **Draft the augmented requirements.** Numbered, "what not how", each with its obligation source (the customer and feature goal; law and regulation; enterprise policy and standards; platform and architecture standards; the business; contracts and counterparties: the six source classes in [01, section 8.1](01-requirements-taxonomy.md)), its evidence link, its confidence and its validator. Where [01](01-requirements-taxonomy.md)'s stated lean is followed, each requirement also carries a quality-attribute class, because the source says where to look and whom to ask while the attribute says how to write and verify it; process obligations (a review, a record, a sign-off) are kept as requirements that point at the gate they trigger, so that mapping finds the gate early. Requirements the person brought and requirements the module added are visibly different.
6. **Check.** Deterministic checks on the artefact: every requirement has a source and a validator; every category was assessed or carries "none, because"; no two requirements contradict; no requirement contradicts a cited policy. Then a fresh-context reader test against the completeness standard, if one exists, with the gaps listed. Checks fail closed: an incomplete record cannot be marked ready.
7. **Route validations.** Each obligation the module inferred becomes the smallest question for its validator ("Does the financial-promotion sign-off apply to a home-screen message that says 'you may be eligible'? The published criteria say a promotion needs it; a service message does not. Which is this?"), with the requirement and the evidence attached. At this stage a person sends. Obligations that fit no category are flagged for curation, not forced into one.
8. **Record.** The intent record is updated: requirements, open questions with owners and blocking flags, decisions with reasons, the questions asked and answered, the "could not find" list. The obligation references it used are noted with the date it read them. Everything it inferred is provisional until a validator confirms it.
9. **Hand over, or interleave.** Validated requirements go to delivery dependency mapping; see section 9 for the options on when.
10. **Readiness is a person's call.** The module reports which checks pass and which validations are open; it never ratifies its own output.

Fictional example, the bank's loan offer ([film brief](../research-dependency-agent/07-film-brief.md)): the marketing product manager brings "a personal-loan offer on the servicing app's home screen, to eligible customers, with a tap-through to apply". Day one, the module reads the last three offer launches and their specs, the promotions policy, the privacy screening criteria and the app platform's content standards. It adds: the wording is a regulated promotion and needs sign-off; targeting existing customers with their own data opens the privacy screen; if eligibility uses a model, a fair-lending and model-risk screen applies; the placement must meet the app's accessibility and content standards; the offer and its wording are records to be retained. It asks the person two things it could not read: whether "pre-approved" is intended, and whether the offer is time-limited. It could not find a published lead time for promotion review, and says so.

### At destination: a run agent in a contracted environment

The same steps, with the environment doing more of the work. Obligations are policy references with owners, checkable criteria and expiry dates (the shaping module's equivalent of the platform card). Classification against them is automatic and monitored by a weekly labelled sample. Validators' past answers are reused: the question about "you may be eligible" was answered once, with the role, the date and the policy reference, and the next offer gets the answer attached without asking. Routine obligations (the accessibility standard applies to any new screen) are attached without a question. Validation requests go to policy owners under the agent's own identity on behalf of the person, and their decisions are recorded automatically. For a defined class of simple intent (a wording change inside an approved pattern; a configuration change with no new data, no new screen and no model), the module ratifies by policy, with the audit record showing which policy, which checks and which identity. Novel obligations, contested classifications and anything outside the defined class still go to people. The completeness standard is a set of evals that delivery reads before it starts.

## 4. What it reads and writes

The module is a reader and writer of the intent lifecycle and the context layer, not the owner of either. [03](03-context-layer-model.md) defines the artefacts and the day-one source map; this is the module's view of them.

| Reads | Day one, from raw sources | At destination |
|---|---|---|
| Past intent of the same kind | Enterprise search over specs, decks, wikis; the ticket tool; past intent records | Indexed intent records through the enterprise data product |
| Obligations | Policy libraries, standards wikis, gate criteria pages, regulator guidance; past specs' obligation lists | Obligation references with owners, checkable criteria and expiry |
| Who validates what | Gate pages, the people directory, who answered past questions | Owner records, verified and dated |
| Prior answers | Chat history, past review threads | Decision records, reusable by reference |

| Writes | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| The augmented, validated requirements | Numbered, sourced, with validators and confidence | The feature's intent record |
| Open questions | With owner, blocking flag, the guess made | The intent record's open-question register |
| Decisions with reasons | Including why an obligation was judged not to apply | Decision records, small and numbered, superseded rather than edited |
| Validation asks and answers | Question, validator, answer, date, policy reference | The intent record, and the context layer's decision records so another feature can reuse the answer |
| Obligation references used | Which policy, which version, read on which date | The context layer; stale references are flagged for the policy owner |
| The "could not find" list | Obligations it expected and found no source for; criteria it could not locate | The intent record, and aggregated as the demand signal |

It does not keep a private store. If the intent record lives in a repository, the module writes files by the convention; if in a tracker or document platform, it writes there. The requirement stays readable without the module.

## 5. What it must not do

- **Not invent an obligation.** No requirement is added without a source: a policy, a criterion, a past validated decision, or the person's own answer. Zero-shot over-prediction from specifications is documented ([LEREDD](../../research/sources/arxiv-darif-leredd-requirement-dependencies.md), independent: F1 0.29 on specific types without retrieved examples).
- **Not conclude an obligation does not apply without citing the criterion.** "Model risk does not apply: no quantitative model is in scope, criterion cited" is allowed; silence is not.
- **Not ratify, and not grade its own work.** The shaper cannot be the ratifier; a fresh context judges the draft; a person decides readiness. Separation of duties by identity, not by prompt.
- **Not replace the person's goal or fill in their answers.** It augments and challenges; it does not decide what the feature is for.
- **Not assess risk.** It classifies which obligations apply and prepares the material; the privacy assessment, the model validation and the promotion sign-off are made by the people whose independence the rules require ([SR 11-7](../../research/sources/federalreserve-sr-11-7-model-risk-guidance.md), [ICO](../../research/sources/ico-when-do-we-need-a-dpia.md), regulator).
- **Not ask what it could have read**, and not ask a validator anything the feature does not need.
- **Not write back as fact.** Everything inferred is provisional until a named validator confirms it, and the record shows which is which.
- **Not advise skipping a gate.** Easier to satisfy, never easier to skip ([02 challenges](../research-dependency-agent/02-challenges.md); the sanction for a skipped change board: [Finansinspektionen](../../research/sources/fi-swedbank-change-management-sanction.md), regulator).
- **Not become a bureaucrat.** Its aggregate output includes which obligations never change a design and which validators never say no, so the organisation can retire what adds nothing.

## 6. Value to each side of the marketplace

**To the person shaping a feature.** The enterprise requirements arrive on day one, not in week nine, with the source and the person who validates each. Questions mid-delivery fall because the answers are in the record. The spec is shorter and evidence-tied. The person spends their time on the goal and the treatment, which is the part only they can do.

**To the owners of policies, standards and gates.** This is the side the marketplace needs and that has the least to gain at first. From day one they get four things: a demand signal (which obligations recur, on which kinds of feature, and which criteria people cannot find); questions that arrive in one shape with the requirement and the evidence attached, rather than a meeting; each question answered once, with their answer reused by name; and the false-positive rate on their own obligations, which tells them where their published criteria are unclear. Later, their criteria become the thing the module checks against, so publishing them in checkable form is rewarded rather than ignored. The evidence that a clearly understood process is itself worth something: respondents with one were 1.8 times more likely to be elite performers, separate from the finding that external approval hurt ([DORA 2019](../../research/sources/dora-state-of-devops-2019.md), independent).

**To the organisation.** The obligation set becomes explicit, in one shape, kept current because a consumer reads it. That is the shaping module's contribution to the legible organisation that [07](07-staged-roadmap.md) describes.

## 7. How it is measured: the four pilot outcomes

| Pilot outcome | Definition for this module | How it is measured | Baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Time to ready** | Working days from the first statement of intent to a person marking the intent ready, with validations closed or owned | Timestamps in the intent record | The same interval for the last ten features of the pilot teams, from ticket and document history |
| **Fewer late surprises, less rework** | Late-discovered obligation rate: obligations added after readiness (by a reviewer, a gate or delivery) as a share of all obligations; count of spec revisions after readiness | The intent record's change history; gate outcomes | The same, reconstructed for past features (expect this to be hard; a prospective control group of non-pilot features is better) |
| **Context compounds** | Share of obligations attached from previously validated references rather than newly inferred (reuse rate); share of inferred obligations a validator confirmed (verification rate); staleness of obligation references | Counted per session; reported monthly | None exists; the first quarter sets it |
| **Owners pull it in unmandated** | Count of policy and gate owners who publish criteria in the module's shape, join sessions or ask for the demand signal without being told to | Observed; a short owner interview each quarter | Zero |

Two cautions. A reconstructed baseline for late surprises will be generous to the status quo, because surprises that were absorbed quietly left no record; the honest comparison is prospective. And time to ready can fall for the wrong reason (fewer obligations attached), so it is read together with the late-surprise rate, never alone.

**The overarching goal, measured inside the four outcomes** ([00](00-overview.md); the full set is in [09, section 10](09-sdd-compatibility.md)):

- *Time to ready* also carries **human effort**: person-hours of the feature owner and validators per feature, from session and ask timestamps plus a short self-report, with every validation a person sent counted rather than hidden. Elapsed time is measured to the delivery-ready slice landing in the code repository, not to a document being marked ready.
- *Fewer late surprises, less rework* also carries **quality** and **compatibility with spec-driven development**: requirements found after handoff (by design, build, review or a gate) per feature; the share of exported requirements with a fit criterion a coding agent turned into a test or check; the share of requirements the spec-driven toolchain consumed without manual translation; and spec commits after the first plan commit, as rework.
- *Context compounds* also carries the **standing layer**: the share of a feature's obligations supplied by generated constitution or steering entries rather than written into its spec.

## 8. How it is evaluated

The model is the risk advisor in the project's position paper: a knowable universe of obligations, each with heuristics objective enough for any capable actor to apply, so that review becomes specification plus classification, and classification can be labelled, sampled and improved by fixing heuristics ([Intent Shaping](../../research/sources/geoff-intent-shaping-well-managed-environment.md), first-party position). Applied to this module:

- **The universe is the requirements taxonomy** in [01](01-requirements-taxonomy.md), on whichever partition the pilot adopts (its lean is source as the primary axis and attribute as the secondary, carried on one record). Each category has heuristics for when an obligation applies, written as checkable items with the published criterion as the source (the privacy regulator's two-of-nine triggers are the pattern, [ICO](../../research/sources/ico-when-do-we-need-a-dpia.md), regulator).
- **Ground truth is what actually applied.** For past features, the obligations that turned out to apply at delivery, labelled by the validators who know; for pilot features, the same, labelled when the feature ships. Subject-matter labelling is the expensive input, so it is sampled: a weekly sample of intents, graded by the owners of the categories.
- **False positives and false negatives are reported per category.** A false positive (an obligation attached that did not apply) costs a validator's time and feeds the bureaucrat risk. A false negative (an obligation missed) is the late surprise the pilot exists to reduce. The two are not weighted the same: in a regulated organisation a false negative at a legal or regulatory obligation costs more than ten false positives at a brand standard, and the per-category report should say which categories are tuned which way. Each error maps to a heuristic to fix, a criterion to clarify with its owner, or a category to add.
- **The spec itself is graded with a rubric built from past artefacts**, not written first: ten to twenty past specs graded pass or fail with reasons by three product people, the criteria emerging from the reasons, kept where reviewers agree ([design guide, section 3](../research-skill-rigidity-spectrum/03-design-guide.md)). A fresh-context judge from a different model family grades the content; a person grades a sample of the judge.
- **The stance is evaluated separately from the output.** Did the person's answers change during the inquiry? Did the open-question count fall? Did they catch a planted weak requirement? ([design guide, section 5](../research-skill-rigidity-spectrum/03-design-guide.md)).
- **Agreement has a ceiling.** Human annotators of requirement dependencies agreed only moderately ([LEREDD](../../research/sources/arxiv-darif-leredd-requirement-dependencies.md), independent: kappa 0.43); expect the same for obligations, measure validator agreement first, and treat it as the ceiling on what the module can be held to.
- **Novel obligations have a curation path.** Anything that fits no category goes to a person, and the taxonomy is revised on a cadence from those cases, by its owner, with a person on the merge.

## 9. The seam with delivery dependency mapping

The unit crossing the seam is a requirement. Shaping produces a requirement with an obligation source and a validator; mapping fills in what satisfies it (an existing platform, a process, a gate, or build) and who must act. The two modules share the intent record and the context layer, so the seam is a field changing state, not a file transfer. [02, section 7](02-structured-inquiry.md) names five handoff points: H1 after the screen (the intent alone crosses, and the universal dependency screen comes back early); H2 per validated requirement; H3 after the whole set is validated; H4 on any change; H5 at readiness. The timing options below are combinations of them.

What comes back across the seam: a requirement whose delivery path changes its wording (the fictional "pre-approved" case: the softer wording is a service message, not a promotion, and needs no eligibility call); a constraint mapping found that shaping did not (the decisioning platform cannot be called in real time, so "show only to eligible customers" needs a different treatment); and an obligation the platform's intake surfaces (the data owner's terms). Each is a reason to re-run the screen on what changed.

When the modules run, laid out as options, not decided:

| Option | What it is | For | Against | What would decide it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sequential** (H3 and H5 only) | Shape fully, ratify, then map | Clean record; the shaper's validators see a finished set; matches the position paper's loop (research, interrogate, spec, then code) | Requirements that change once their delivery path is known are found after ratification; two passes over the same owners | A low rate of mapping-driven requirement changes in the pilot |
| **Interleaved** (H2 per requirement, with H1 as an early universal screen) | Map each requirement as it is validated; let the delivery path feed back before the set is ratified | Rabbit holes found before commitment (the Shape Up breadboarding pattern, where a senior engineer in the room is the completeness test, [stories, intent-shaping options](../../options/DIMENSIONS.md#sub-choice-intent-shaping-mode--who-shapes-intent-and-how), anecdote); fewer late surprises by construction | A longer, messier shaping session; the person may be asked platform questions before the goal is settled | A high rate of mapping-driven changes; evidence that the person tolerates the longer session |
| **Continuous through delivery** (H4 stays on after ratification) | Both modules stay on through build and release, re-screening on every change to the spec, the map or the environment | Catches the mid-delivery requirement and the platform that moved; the record stays true | Noise if re-screening is not targeted; the modules become a standing presence that needs a steward | Evidence that most late surprises arise after ratification rather than before |
| **Mapping-first** (H1 before any shaping) | Run the universal screen on the raw goal before requirements are written, so the anchors and the long-lead gates are known first | The gates, funding and calendar facts that decide phase boundaries arrive before the requirements they shape; cheap, because the screen reads only the context | Finds the universal dependencies, not the requirement-specific ones; on its own it is a prelude to one of the other three, not an alternative to them | Long-lead gates on the pilot's common paths; it is compatible with all three forms above, so little decides against it |

A stated lean, with its reason: start the pilot interleaved, because the pilot's job is to find the rate of mapping-driven requirement changes, and only the interleaved form measures it without a second pass. The lean would change if the pilot teams find the longer session intolerable, or if the rate turns out to be low. [02](02-structured-inquiry.md) sharpens the choice: because H1 is cheap and compatible with every form, the real decision is between H2 (per requirement) and H3 (after the set), and the pilot should measure how often a requirement changes after mapping, how often a validator is asked twice about the same requirement, and the elapsed time from goal to readiness under each.

The register's open question remains open: whether the dependency and open-questions check is a distinct job or part of spec review ([spec-standard, Q14](../../options/DIMENSIONS.md#sub-choice-spec-standard--what-counts-as-agent-ready-intent)). The seam described here is compatible with either answer; the packaging options in [00](00-overview.md) say what each would cost.

## 10. Open questions

1. What is the universal question set every intent must answer here, with a named source per answer, and which answers can the module find from day-one sources? (Register: intent-shaping-mode Q1 and Q2; answered by a workshop with pilot teams plus a review of past specs, then the pilot.)
2. Which partition of the requirements categories, by obligation source or by quality attribute or a hybrid, gives the fewest items landing in two classes or none on real features? ([01](01-requirements-taxonomy.md); answered by the four fictional features there, then the pilot's first ten.)
3. Can product and engineering agree one completeness standard, and can an eval judge it at the level validators agree with each other? (Register: spec-standard Q2; answered by grading past specs first.)
4. What is the validator agreement ceiling per category, and which categories have criteria clear enough to classify against? (Answered by the first labelled samples.)
5. Which class of intent is simple enough to ratify by policy at destination, who defines the class, and does audit accept a ratification with an agent identity on it? (Answered by risk, audit and the second line; the roadmap's run stage depends on it.)
6. Does the up-level stance (interrogate and withhold) pay in product work, where no evidence exists, or does delivering a draft first produce better specs with less friction? (Design guide's proposed test; answered by running the same skill both ways on five live intents.)
7. Does a task agent beat a skill in a general harness on this workload, on quality and review effort? (The bake-off; [00](00-overview.md).)
8. How often does mapping change a requirement, and does that decide the sequencing (section 9)? (Answered by the pilot, interleaved.)
9. What happens when the person is not present: does the module stop, or run to a stated floor and flag it? (The design guide's headless fallback; a policy decision.)
10. Whose identity does the module use when it asks a validator, and can a validator who is not in the session answer from where they are? ([Shared-agents synthesis](../research-shared-persistent-agents/01-synthesis.md), C8 and the identity condition.)
11. Can one agent identity serve many features, with a named sponsor per feature, rather than one identity per module or per team? A design direction to explore, not a pilot decision: it would give validators one recognisable sender and give audit one accountable person per feature. It needs the identity platform to carry a per-feature sponsor on each action ([agent identity with a business sponsor per agent](../../research/sources/microsoft-learn-agent-365-identity.md), first-party, is the nearest shipped model; the per-feature variant is unsourced). (Answered by identity and audit owners, then a prototype.)
