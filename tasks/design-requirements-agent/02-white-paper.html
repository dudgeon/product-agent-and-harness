The requirements agent

An agent that turns a product idea into the complete, sourced set of requirements a change must meet to ship in a large, platform-centric, regulated organisation.

Part of the product agent and harness project. Companion page: the four-layer model. The organisation described is generic and the worked example is fictional.

Summary

Every change carries two sets of requirements. The first is what the person proposing it wants. The second is what the organisation requires of any change of that kind: law and regulation, enterprise policy and controls, the standards any build must meet, the business's constraints, counterparties' terms, and the monitoring, reporting and records that follow launch. In a large regulated organisation the first set is written early. The second is found late, one domain at a time, by whichever reviewer happens to look.

The requirements agent finds the second set at the start. It takes a high-level idea in any form, reads the organisation's context, screens the change against a finite list of requirement domains, asks the person only what it could not read, and produces one requirements register in which every line has a source, a validator and a test. Every domain ends in requirements or in a recorded reason it does not apply, so a missed domain shows up as a failed check before anyone builds.

It is implementation-agnostic. It states what must be true and leaves which platforms, data and processes will make it true to the dependency agent, which reads the register next. It never assesses risk, never ratifies its own output, and treats everything it infers as provisional until a named person confirms it.

1. The problem

Consider an organisation with thousands of people doing product management, each shaping intent in their own way. Solutions are composed from internal platforms owned across the company, some of them hard to find or evaluate. The organisation is well run and heavily regulated, so a change can bring in obligations from a dozen domains: consumer protection, privacy, fair treatment, financial promotions, model risk, third-party risk, information security, accessibility, records retention, operational resilience, complaints handling, regulatory reporting, and the controls and monitoring that evidence each.

No one person carries all of that. The product manager knows their customer and their goal. Each reviewer knows one domain. The result is a familiar pattern: a requirements document that is complete on the feature's own behaviour and silent on two or three enterprise domains, which are then added by a reviewer at a gate, by an engineer mid-build, or after launch when a monitoring or reporting obligation surfaces. Each late addition costs rework, delay and the reviewer's time, and in a regulated setting a missed obligation can also be a finding.

The project's earlier work describes the current state in first-party terms: refinement is the bottleneck, done bespoke each time, with context scattered across private documents and the people who know, and only a sprint or two of ready intent ahead of delivery [1]. The independent evidence is stronger on the dependency side than on the requirements side: the UK regulator's review of over a million technology changes found that a project depending on other projects was the most consistent factor in failure, and asked firms to invest in discovery before designing change [2]. No independent study measuring late-discovered obligations in product work was found; the pilot proposed in section 10 would produce the first numbers.

2. The job, and its boundary

In one line: from what I want to what must be true for this to ship here, with a source on every line.

The requirements agent
TakesA high-level idea in whatever form it exists (a paragraph, a deck, a ticket, a prototype, a voice note) and the requirements the person already knows.
ProducesOne requirements register: the feature's own requirements and every enterprise obligation that applies, each with its source, source class, attribute class, fit criterion, validator, phase and status. Around it: the change profile, open questions, a decision log, validation packets, a readiness statement and a could-not-find list. The register is rendered into the forms people already use (section 8).
ServesAnyone doing the product task: product managers, business analysts, designers whose prototype is about to become the spec, engineers handed a ticket that says what but not what else.
Is notA PRD generator; a risk assessor; an approver. It classifies which obligations apply and prepares the material. The privacy assessment, the model validation, the promotion sign-off and the decision that the intent is ready stay with the people whose independence the rules require.

Implementation-agnostic, precisely

The boundary with the dependency agent needs a rule, because some enterprise requirements are about the build. The rule used here: a standard that any implementation must meet is a requirement; which platform, capability, data set or process meets it is a dependency.

Belongs in the registerBelongs to the dependency agent
"Decisions about who sees the offer are logged and retained for the records period."Which decisioning platform logs them, and how to onboard to it.
"The journey meets the organisation's accessibility level."Which design-system components already meet it.
"Marketing opt-outs are honoured before display."Which consent service holds the opt-outs, and the access request.
"The service meets the resilience tier for a customer-facing channel."Which hosting pattern provides that tier.
A mandate to use a named strategic platform, recorded as a constraint with its sourceEverything about that platform: capability, intake, lead time, owner.

Process obligations (a review that must happen, a record that must be filed, a sign-off that must be obtained) stay in the register as requirements that point at the review they trigger. That puts the privacy assessment on the page on day one, next to the requirement that caused it, rather than leaving it for the dependency agent's screen to discover later.

3. How it prevents the missed domain

The design rests on one idea from the project's position paper on risk review: in a well-run regulated organisation, the universe of enterprise obligations is knowable, each has heuristics for when it applies, and those heuristics are objective enough for any capable actor to apply. Review then becomes a specification problem plus a classification problem [3]. Four mechanisms follow from it.

  1. A finite list, closed explicitly. The agent screens every change against a fixed list of requirement domains, keyed to the enterprise risk taxonomy and the published triggers of each review. Every domain the screen opens must end with requirements or with "none, because" and the criterion. Silence is a failed check. This turns "did we think of everything" from a judgment into a count.
  2. Precedent, including what was added late. Before asking anything, the agent finds features of the same kind and copies what they ended up needing, with particular weight on what reviewers added after readiness. The last feature's late surprise becomes this feature's day-one requirement.
  3. Checks that fail closed, and a critic with fresh context. Deterministic checks confirm that every requirement has a source, a validator and a fit criterion and that nothing blocking is unrouted. A separate reader with no memory of the session then grades the register against the completeness standard and lists what it cannot find.
  4. A loop that keeps the list current. Every obligation added after readiness, by a reviewer, a gate or delivery, is recorded with the stage it was found. It is proposed as an addition to the baseline for that kind of feature, so the next feature starts with it.

The agent standardises the output while leaving the person's method alone. A product manager can bring a one-line idea or a forty-page document; the register that comes out has the same shape, the same domains and the same checks every time. That is what lets thousands of people with different habits produce comparable, reviewable intent.

4. A session

The phases run in a fixed order. What happens inside each is the model's judgment. This is the profile the project's skill-spectrum work recommends for shaping: rigid at gathering and classification, loose at interrogation, with decisions kept by a person [4]. The worked example is a fictional bank adding a personal-loan offer to the home screen of its servicing app, for existing customers who are eligible, with a tap-through to apply.

PhaseWhat happensIn the example
1 · FrameRestates the goal for correction; asks for the evidence and measure behind it; answers the screening facets (does it show, decide, use data, target a group, use a model, touch a channel with its own rules, involve a third party, change an operating process, need measuring); finds precedent.Three past offer launches are found. In one, the promotion sign-off was added in week seven. A model's use is unknown and becomes the first question.
2 · DiscoverScreens each domain against its published triggers; derives requirements from each applicable obligation; asks the person the questions the context could not answer, one at a time, each with a guess; explores the cases the idea skipped.Opens promotions, marketing consent, privacy, fair lending, accessibility, records retention and complaints. Asks whether "pre-approved" is intended. Adds suppression for customers in collections or flagged as vulnerable.
3 · ShapeWrites each requirement as one checkable statement with a fit criterion; shows which scope choices pull in which obligations; reconciles conflicts; traces each requirement to source, control, validator and test."You may be eligible" keeps phase one a promotion; "pre-approved" would add a credit decision and its reviews. A rules-based audience avoids a model-risk review. The PM picks the first of each.
4 · VerifyRuns the completeness checks, which fail closed; a fresh-context critic grades the register; inferred requirements are grouped into one packet per validator, with the evidence attached. At first a person sends the packets.The check fails on records retention: opened, no requirement, no reason. Fixed. One packet goes to the promotions reviewer, one to the privacy office, none to model risk.
5 · Hand off and learnRenders the register for the dependency agent, the spec-driven toolchain, the PRD and the review intake forms; re-screens only what a later change touches; proposes what was learned as context for the next feature.The dependency agent reports eligibility cannot be checked in real time on the home screen; one requirement is reworded and only its domain re-screened. The promotions reviewer's ruling on the wording is proposed as a prior ruling with a twelve-month expiry.

Readiness is a person's call. The agent reports which checks pass, what is validated and what is open; it never marks its own output ready.

5. What it reads: unstructured context

Unstructured context is where the agent looks. It is good for finding what might apply and poor as proof that something does. Anything the agent takes from it is marked provisional and, where a governed record exists, checked against that record.

Access follows the person. The agent sees what the person asking could see, and reads public support channels for their own topic only. Private messages stay private, and some jurisdictions restrict mining employee communications at all [5].

6. What it cites: governed data products

These are the authoritative, owned, versioned records specific to requirements work. They are what let the agent say "this applies, here is the record" instead of "this probably applies". Most large regulated organisations already hold several of them, often in a governance, risk and compliance system or a policy management system, though rarely in a form an agent can query by trigger.

Three readings of the inventory

  • Four of the seventeen are made by the agent's own work. Intent records, prior rulings, feature-kind baselines and the pattern library grow from validated sessions. They do not need a programme to build them first; they need an owner to verify what the agent proposes.
  • The smallest useful start is four records. The review and gate catalogue with published triggers, the policy and standards register, the decision-rights register and the glossary let the agent screen, cite, route and avoid the word that opens a review by accident. Everything else can begin as Layer 1 context, marked provisional.
  • The could-not-find list says what to build next. Across many features, the obligations the agent expected and could not source, and the review criteria nobody published, are a ranked demand signal for which data products, and which fields in them, would remove the most questions.

What the requirements agent does not need: platform and capability catalogues, API registries, configuration databases, intake queues and lead times. Those are the dependency agent's inputs.

8. Artifacts

There is one source of truth, the requirements register, and many renderings of it. That keeps the PRD, the tickets, the review forms and the spec files from drifting apart, and lets people who never open the register work in the forms they already use.

Generated by the agent

  • Intent brief: goal, problem, customers, outcome, measure, scope, non-goals.
  • Change profile: the facets answered and every domain opened or closed, with reasons. This is the artifact that makes a missed domain visible.
  • Requirements register: every requirement with ID, statement, source class, attribute class, fit criterion, source, validator, phase and status. The six source classes are the feature's own goal; law and regulation; enterprise policy and standards; standards any build must meet; the business; contracts and counterparties [7].
  • Acceptance criteria: scenarios and fit criteria a reviewer or a coding agent can test.
  • Traceability matrix: requirement to obligation to control to validator to evidence.
  • Open questions and assumptions, each with an owner, a blocking flag and the guess made.
  • Decision log, numbered and superseded rather than edited, including every "does not apply, because".
  • Validation packets, one per validator.
  • Readiness statement, for the person who ratifies.
  • Could-not-find list, per feature and aggregated.
  • Dependency handoff: the validated register as the dependency agent reads it.
  • Spec-driven export: feature spec files in the layout the team's tool reads, one checkable statement per requirement with a stable ID, and standing obligations as repository rules so they are not restated in every spec [8].

Existing artifacts it augments

  • PRD, epics and stories in the document and tracker the team already uses.
  • Review intake drafts: privacy screening, new product approval, model risk intake, third-party intake, security threat-model kickoff, financial promotion submission, accessibility plan. Drafted from the register; submitted and decided by people.
  • Control mapping input for the risk and control self-assessment.
  • Monitoring and measurement plan: success metrics and the experiment, plus the risk indicators, surveillance, regulatory reports, attestations and records the change feeds, and the metrics that would trigger withdrawing it [10]. This is where the obligations most often found after launch are put on the page before it.
  • Disclosures and communications: the customer-facing wording obligations and who approves each.
  • Launch readiness checklist: what must be evidenced before release.
  • Context write-backs: proposed prior rulings, baseline additions, stale-policy flags and glossary terms, each sent to the owner of the record it would change.

9. Rules it holds to

  1. Read before asking. A person is asked only what the context cannot answer, and the smallest form of the question.
  2. No requirement without a source: a governed record, a cited document, or a named person's answer. Language models over-predict at this kind of task when they work from the specification alone [6].
  3. No domain closed in silence. "Does not apply" always cites the criterion.
  4. Provisional until verified. The record shows which entries a named person has confirmed, kept separate from the agent's own confidence.
  5. Classify, never assess. Risk assessment, validation and sign-off stay with the people whose independence the rules require [9].
  6. Never ratify, never self-grade. The drafter cannot be the ratifier, and the critic runs in a fresh context. Separation of duties is enforced by identity, not by prompt.
  7. Easier to meet, never easier to skip. It can show a simpler way to satisfy an obligation. It never advises skipping one that applies.
  8. Answer once. A validator's answer is written where the next feature will find it, with role, date, policy reference and expiry.
  9. Do not become the bureaucrat. Its aggregate reporting includes the obligations that never change a design and the reviews that never say no, so the organisation can retire what adds nothing. A baseline item earns its place from a past late addition or incident, as Google requires of its launch checklist, and low-risk changes get a shorter path [13].

Time to ready is read together with the late-discovered rate, never alone, because it also falls when fewer obligations are attached. A baseline reconstructed from past features will flatter the status quo, because absorbed surprises left no record; a prospective comparison with non-pilot features is the honest one.

Evaluation

Ground truth is what actually applied: for past features, the obligations that turned out to apply, labelled by the validators who know; for pilot features, the same at ship. False positives and false negatives are reported per domain and weighted differently: a missed legal obligation costs more than ten unnecessary brand checks, and each domain's tuning should say which way it leans. Human agreement sets a ceiling: annotators of requirement dependencies agreed only moderately in the one study found (kappa 0.43) [6], so validator agreement is measured first and the agent is held to it, not above it. The register itself is graded with a rubric built from ten to twenty past specs graded by product people, criteria kept where the graders agree [4].

No organisation found publishes an agent that does this whole job. The pieces exist, mostly as human processes, and each one shaped part of the design. Evidence labels follow the project's convention.

12. Open questions

These are left open. Each says what would answer it.

  1. The universal domain list. Which domains does every change in this organisation screen, with what triggers and which owner? Answered by a workshop with the owners of the anchor reviews and a review of the last year's late additions.
  2. The partition of the register. Source class first with attribute second, or the reverse, or by validator? The project's lean is source first, because it says where to look and whom to ask on day one [7]; the share of requirements with a verified source chain after a quarter would confirm or overturn it.
  3. The stance. Does interrogating first and drafting later produce better registers than drafting first and asking second? No evidence exists for product work; running the same intents both ways would answer it.
  4. The class of change simple enough to ratify by policy, and whether audit accepts a ratification under an agent identity. Answered by risk, audit and the second line.
  5. How often the delivery path changes a requirement. If often, the two agents should interleave per requirement; if rarely, they can run in sequence. Answered by running the pilot interleaved and counting.
  6. Packaging. One agent with two modules, two agents sharing a record, or skills in a general harness. The project's handoff research and a bake-off against a no-agent baseline would decide it.
