## Relevance to the project

Andrew Chen's own preview of the introduction to his book names the problem the dependency-mapping agent faces: a networked product with too few participants gives each new one little reason to stay, and "if it's not overcome quickly, a new product will die." His workplace-chat example is the closest of his cases to an internal tool. It frames the cold start as a race against attrition, which bears on how long single-player usefulness must carry feature teams before the owner side arrives. Practitioner essay; claims are drawn from his Uber and investing experience, not measured.

## Themes

- **two-sided-cold-start**: the cold start is the first stage of a networked product; without enough users on both sides, users leave and the product dies (introduction). Anecdote.
- **two-sided-cold-start**: workplace example: "If a workplace chat product doesn't have all your colleagues on it, it won't be adopted at the office" (introduction). Anecdote.

## Key points

- Defines the cold start problem as the launch phase where a networked product lacks the users it needs to be useful (introduction).
- Uses Uber to describe two-sided balance: too few drivers and riders leave; too much surge and riders leave; too little and drivers go offline (introduction, Uber anecdote).
- Argues "network effects" is used loosely in pitches and lacks agreed metrics (introduction).
- The preview does not cover atomic networks, the hard side or tipping points; those are in later chapters (see [Solve a Hard Problem (Tinder)](andrewchen-solve-a-hard-problem-hard-side.md)).

## Notable quotes

- "This is the Cold Start Problem, and if it's not overcome quickly, a new product will die." (introduction)
