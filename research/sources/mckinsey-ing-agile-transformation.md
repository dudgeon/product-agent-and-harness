## Relevance to the project
ING Netherlands' leaders describe moving 3,500 head-office staff into about 350 nine-person squads in 13 tribes in 2015 to "minimize handovers and bureaucracy", with quarterly business reviews in which each tribe states which other tribes it must link up with: a dependency-declaration ritual. Reported benefits are qualitative except release cadence (every two to three weeks instead of five to six launches a year). First-party, 15 months in, in a consultancy publication; INSEAD measurement mentioned but not reported.

## Themes
- coordination-cost-and-waiting: agility is "about minimizing handovers and bureaucracy"; the old organisation had "managers controlling the handovers and slowing down collaboration" (Jacobs, Schlatmann).
- dependency-value-evidence: in the QBR "each tribe writes down what it achieved ... and, in that context, which other tribe or squad it will need to link up with"; QBR documents are open to all tribes (Schlatmann).
- process-governance-load: legal, finance and operational risk "are not part of a squad per se, as they need to be independent, but a squad can call on them"; regulators "were at times worried that agile meant freedom and chaos" (Schlatmann).

## Key points
- Reported impact: releases every two to three weeks rather than five to six times a year; customer-satisfaction and employee-engagement scores "up multiple points"; INSEAD engaged to measure as a neutral outsider.
- Risk named by the CIO: autonomous squads "go in different directions if you don't align squads, say, every quarter or six months".
- All head-office staff reapplied for roles; nearly 40% of the 2,500 changed position.
- The model was inspired by Spotify and others; support functions (HR, finance, risk) were left out of tribes at first.
