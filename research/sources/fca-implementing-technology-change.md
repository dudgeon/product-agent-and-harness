## Relevance to the project
The UK FCA's multi-firm review of over 1 million production changes made in 2019 by 23 banks, wholesale firms and insurers found that the single most consistent risk factor for a failed change was "a project ... dependent on other projects delivering their objectives", and that CABs approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed, some rejecting none all year. It is a regulator's own evidence that cross-project dependency is the core delivery risk in regulated firms, that the classic change gate often rubber-stamps rather than assures, and that the regulator wants firms to "invest in estate discovery to understand dependencies before designing change". Of all sources in this sweep it is the strongest single anchor for the dependency agent's problem statement in regulated organisations.

## Themes
- dependency-knowledge-carriers / coordination-cost-and-waiting: the most consistent risk factor was when "a project is dependent on other projects delivering their objectives", requiring tight coordination and changes completed in tandem; projects on new technology, changing legacy systems, or relying heavily on third parties were also high risk; being classed "major" was a less consistent factor (Findings: high-risk projects).
- human-review-gates: "CABs approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed"; in some firms a CAB had not rejected a single change in 2019; many CABs act more as "flight control" than assurance; recommends careful selection of CAB members and subject matter experts (Findings: CABs).
- process-governance-load: firms with governance established for more than a year had a lower proportion of change-related incidents; firms managing change risk continuously within day-to-day project management had higher success rates than those using periodic or ad hoc approaches (Findings: governance).
- regulatory-drivers-of-process: good practice includes estate discovery to understand dependencies before designing change, especially with legacy systems, and contractual visibility of third-party changes (Good practices).
- risk-sloped-review: smaller, more frequent releases and agile delivery were associated with higher success rates; major changes failed at 3.8% versus 1.6% for all changes (Findings: release frequency; major changes).

## Key points
- Scope: 23 firms (8 wholesale, 8 retail, 7 insurance); over 1 million production changes in 2019; nearly 20,000 change-related incidents analysed; questionnaires and workshops.
- 1.6% of changes caused an incident: over 13,767 incidents in 2019, 14% customer-facing. Change caused 24% of high-severity incidents.
- Over 90% of firms still relied on legacy infrastructure or applications for production services; more legacy meant more failed and emergency changes.
- Over 30% of development activity was delivered by third parties; third-party incidents were 18% of FCA-reported incidents in 2019, 22% of those due to third-party change; most firms did not effectively track third-party changes.
- Over 33,000 emergency changes in 2019 (3-5% of volume; some firms near 20%).
- Most firms deployed core-system changes between monthly and quarterly; 84% used DevOps in some form but only 13% for all software delivery; weekly-or-faster deployers more often used automated deployment (78%) and microservices (89%).
