## Relevance to the project
The strongest general evidence on what happens when work becomes observable: a meta-analysis of electronic performance monitoring finds monitoring is stressful for workers regardless of its characteristics, monitoring without an explicit purpose does not raise performance, and only transparent, less invasive monitoring goes with better attitudes. Shared, persistent agent sessions and trace mining (catalogue items 6 and 7) make work visible in exactly this way, so the paper supports the "privacy and chilling effects" objection alongside the Bernstein transparency-paradox note, and gives design conditions: say what the traces are for, keep it transparent, keep it narrow.

## Themes
- public-by-default-agent-work: regardless of monitoring characteristics, EPM tends to be stressful for workers; transparent, less invasive monitoring "can expect more positive attitudes from workers" (abstract, NFW 2020)
- agent-failure-modes: monitoring without an explicit purpose was not associated with any increase in performance, so trace collection "because we can" buys stress without output (abstract)

## Key points
- Moderators: transparency, invasiveness, and whether monitoring has an explicit purpose.
- Authors argue HR principles, not technical capability, should decide monitoring practice.
- Published version: Personnel Psychology, DOI 10.1111/peps.12514 (accepted 2022); search summaries report 94 independent samples, N = 23,461 (not verified by me).
- A separate meta-analysis (Siegel, König and Lazar 2022, Computers in Human Behavior Reports) reportedly found r = -0.10 with job satisfaction, r = .11 with stress, r = -0.01 with performance (search summary, not opened).
