## Relevance to the project
The best measured figures on how fast an openly edited store repairs damage, weighted by how many readers saw the damage: about half of damage incidents were repaired by the first or second page view, but 11% survived more than 100 views. It bears on "trust by use with corrections" and on a time-to-correction criterion: correction speed scales with readership, so a busy store corrects itself and a quiet one does not. This is a secondary report of the paper; the paper's PDF was not reachable.

## Themes
- context-trust-mechanisms: probability that a typical page view shows a damaged article estimated at 0.0037 (under half a percent); about half of damage incidents repaired on the first or second view; 11% persisted beyond an estimated 100 views (Signpost summary of the paper's results).

## Key points
- Method: one month of sampled Wikimedia request logs (every tenth request) joined to edit histories; damage approximated by edits later reverted, which also catches content disputes (method paragraph). The measure, "persistent word views", weights each word by how often readers saw it.
- Measurable definition the project can borrow: exposure-weighted damage = views of a record while it was wrong / total views; time to correction counted in reads, not days.
- The damage-view probability was rising until a break around June 2006 that the authors attribute to anti-vandalism bots (trend paragraph): automated reversion measurably shortened exposure.
- The top 10% of editors by edit count contributed 86% of persistent word views. The lead author later said on the talk page that the abstract's "overwhelming majority from frequent editors" claim was not supported in its strong form (talk-page caveat as summarised).
- "Essentially no correlation between views and edits" in the logs: being read is not the same as being maintained.
