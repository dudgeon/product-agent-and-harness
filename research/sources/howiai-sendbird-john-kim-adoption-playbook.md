## Relevance to the project
A CEO describing how his own company turned AI adoption into an internal product: a "quest" marketplace where anyone posts an automation need and engineers or AI agents pick it up (agents now read the request, write a PRD and start coding), a pre-vetted, compliant app template so non-engineers can ship to production, a company skills/plugin marketplace, and a token dashboard with five tiers that managers use for coaching. It is first-party evidence for the adoption-model dimension (adoption as a product with a dedicated team reporting to the CEO) and for "the paved road" as the governance answer to shadow building. It touches intent shaping only lightly: the quest is a lightweight intake artifact that an agent turns into a PRD.

## Themes
- enablement-by-product: Vo frames it as "think of their team as a product"; Kim built the Automators platform, quests, guides updated "pretty much on a daily basis", and an app template (8:18-10:51); Vo: the platform "sits next to your normal roadmap not in your normal roadmap" (30:01).
- non-engineer-builders: marketing built a Stripe-integrated swag store "in a matter of a day or two" without engineering (4:09); marketing runs its own suite of internal tools (plan calendar, account-based marketing, competitor review, a campaign "buzz board") used daily (18:56); weekly all-hands demos are "almost never actually the engineering team" (12:29).
- scoped-governance-containers: app template with authentication and environments pre-set, "fully compliant. All the security is already pre-built in" (9:51); a weekly task force with CTO, engineering and Infosec vets the stack, logging and compliance in advance (14:56). Quests display a risk rating, weeks saved and beneficiary (Vo describing the screen, 10:51).
- skills-marketplace: anyone can publish a plugin (a collection of skills) or single skills per function (sales MEDDPICC advisor, recruiting, design); motive is that people across functions were building the same app or skill, "co-evolve rather than people operating in silos" (16:19).
- org-learning-loop: skills spread organically through Slack and Wednesday stage demos ("There are usually some skills involved", 17:33); quest completers earn experience points redeemable for gift cards, tea with an executive, or a company demo slot (12:29).
- outcome-measurement-gap: Sendbird measures tokens, not outcomes; Kim rejects tokens-as-performance ("not part of the performance review") citing lines-of-code gaming (24:28); five tiers from beginner to "AI god" (>100M tokens/day); the goal is a smooth curve so agents work through weekends and vacations (24:28-25:58).
- pm-persona-role-expansion: new role "AI engineer for internal operations" reporting to CEO and chief of staff (14:56); job descriptions rewritten to lower tenure requirements and select for curiosity, agency and energy (35:05).

## Key points
- Quest flow: quest giver posts a need (e.g. finance automating AR/AP, a customer account lookup); others team up; output is a code repo or a skill; new feature: AI reads the spec, writes a PRD and starts coding alongside humans (8:18-9:51).
- Adoption pressure is both top-down (executive one-on-ones: "we noticed that you haven't been spending any tokens") and bottom-up (curious people find skills) (17:33).
- Tool split is organic: Claude Code leads overall, Codex is heavier among people working on the legacy chat infrastructure (300M+ monthly active users); Kim says the Claude Code share fell from about 75-80% to two-thirds within a month (24:28, 29:43).
- Kim personally averages 30-50M tokens/day, peak ~200M; top consumers are the CTO and chief architect, which Kim calls the strongest adoption signal (27:43, 36:38).
- Organization sits at "stage two and stage three" on their own maturity scale: "a lot of automation, but not fully automated" (25:58).
- Personal: an open-source "Gardener" that tends an Obsidian knowledge base daily (enrich, cross-link, fix); AI-built personal learning sites (31:00-35:05).
