## Relevance to the project
A revelatory case study of a four-year, roughly EUR 140 million public-sector program (12 Scrum teams, 175 people at peak, two-thirds external consultants) shows that dependencies were coordinated through far more mechanisms than any framework prescribes, and that most of the real work happened through people, boards and unscheduled conversation, not the plan. It also records the failure of the spreadsheet master plan (no overview, version confusion) and its replacement by an issue tracker holding 300 epics and 2,500 stories. It is the best evidence in the library of what dependency management in a regulated public body looks like from the inside.

## Themes
- dependency-types-and-practices: uses Malone and Crowston's definition of coordination as "management of interdependencies between activities" and Van de Ven's three modes (impersonal, personal individual, personal group) (§Background, Table 1); finds 19 mechanisms across those modes (Table 3).
- dependency-knowledge-carriers: the matrix gave each feature team a technical architect (50% design, 50% development) and a functional architect who also served the architecture and business projects (Table 2, §Case); "management by walking around" and project managers who said "this problem I know was addressed by another team two iterations ago, let us get Ola over here" (§Results, Individual mode).
- process-artifacts-as-coordination: the master plan began as a spreadsheet and was replaced about a year in by Jira because of two problems: no overview at that size, and "it was difficult to know what the latest version of the spreadsheet was" (§Impersonal mode). All process descriptions, guidelines and checklists lived in a mandatory wiki.
- coordination-cost-and-waiting: "In the business project, much of the work concentrated on managing dependencies, 'there were dependencies throughout the program'" (§Group mode); separate meetings were held "to identify dependencies in tasks before work was assigned to teams".

## Key points
- Case "Omega": a public department of about 380 employees serving 950,000 customers; new IT platform required by public reform; January 2008 to March 2012; final budget about EUR 140 million; 175 people at peak, of whom 100 were external consultants from five companies; 12 feature teams in three subprojects (6, 3, 3) on three-week iterations (§Case).
- Matrix structure: business, architecture, test and development projects; feature teams lent people to the other three (Figure 1, §Case).
- Release phases: analysis of needs; solution description; construction (five to seven three-week iterations); approval, then acceptance by the department's operational IT section (a governance handoff) (§Case).
- Group mechanisms: Metascrum twice weekly for program management (risks and "high-level" obstacles); Scrum of Scrums per subproject (one went from daily to three times a week); experience forum, lunch seminars, technical corner, open space (§Results).
- Architecture meetings coordinated which teams touched which part of the codebase: "this is our central area of work this period, so please limit work in that area".
- Teams kept duplicate physical boards even though the tracker was mandatory: "Changing the status in the issue tracker does not bring an applause."
- Cites Paasivaara et al. (2012): Scrum of Scrums alone "did not lead to satisfactory coordination" (§Background).
- Main finding: mechanisms changed over time, and the program relied on feedback-based (personal) coordination far beyond practitioner advice.
