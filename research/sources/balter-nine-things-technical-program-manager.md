## Relevance to the project
A former GitHub product manager who helped found its TPM organisation lists nine things a TPM does; coordinating "dependencies and other inter-related efforts across teams" is item one, and much of the rest (capturing work, tracking status, reporting with "no surprises", forcing tough conversations, being the catch-all owner) is the human labour a dependency agent would sit beside. Useful as a practitioner definition of the role from a large software company, with the caveat that GitHub is not a regulated enterprise.

## Themes
- dependency-knowledge-carriers: the TPM is "the 'catch all' for any problem, task, or deliverable that doesn't have another logical owner", including tedious manual spreadsheet updates and repackaged status reports (item 9).
- dependency-types-and-practices: item 2, "capture and track the work": scope, timing, ordering, owners and external dependencies, then tracking each workstream and deliverable's status; plan and reality must stay in sync. Item 3 reframes the work as "risks and mitigations" rather than problems and solutions.
- process-artifacts-as-coordination: item 7: "Everything (and I do mean everything) is documented, discoverable, and socialized"; the TPM surfaces pending decisions and makes sure decision-makers have what they need.

## Key points
- Item 4: the main deliverable is "to ensure that there are no surprises"; execution should seem "boring, or at the very least predictable". Because TPMs own execution rather than outcomes, they can report unvarnished status.
- Item 5: relationships must be "relational, not transactional": "Don't be the person your colleague only hears from when you need something." (A note for any agent that only ever chases.)
- Item 6: TPMs force tough conversations as a neutral third party when priorities conflict.
- Item 8: "boundaryless engagement", going wherever the work is regardless of org lines.
