## Relevance to the project
The securities self-regulator's description of how broker-dealers actually run new-product vetting: a standardised written proposal, a preliminary compliance/legal triage of whether it is "new" and what review level applies, a cross-functional working group, a senior committee decision (approve, disapprove, table or approve with conditions), and post-approval review. It is the clearest public shape of a new product approval (NPA) workflow and includes a 14-question minimum interview, which is close to what a requirements agent's screening stage would ask. It bears on how the agent's intake and triage should work and on what "conditional approval" means as a requirement type.

## Themes
- launch-readiness-review: review components observed across firms: standardised proposal with assigned business-unit ownership; preliminary assessment; detailed committee review; formal decision by a senior new products committee; post-approval follow-up (Review Process section).
- risk-review-as-classification: firms need practical criteria for what is "new" or a "material modification" (new to market or firm, first sale to retail, material changes in risk, structure or fees, significant operational or system changes, new conflicts); when unsure, err to full review (Written Procedures).
- process-governance-load: compliance and legal should be involved early, ideally embedded, so they are part of the team rather than a late obstacle; operations, sales and supervisors take part before approval to check systems, training and controls (Who participates).
- regulatory-drivers-of-process: procedures sit under supervision rules (then NASD Rule 3010); the practices are "not designed as a safe harbor".

## Key points
- The minimum questions (paraphrased by section): intended audience and who should not be offered it; investment objective and whether simpler or cheaper products meet it; assumptions and return drivers; investor risks; costs and fees and their transparency; firm and rep compensation and conflicts; novel legal, tax, market or credit risks; complexity and its effect on suitability and training; marketing materials and disclosures; qualifications of the assessors; training needs; distribution (own or third parties) and liquidity; whether current systems support the product; whether the product triggers additional rules.
- Approvals are often conditional (limit sales to customers with given objectives or net worth, cap concentration, require training). Conditions must be feasible operationally before approval and do not replace suitability analysis.
- Post-approval review for complex, conditionally approved or assumption-sensitive products runs six months to a year: complaints, training, compliance with conditions, re-assessed suitability, review before lifting restrictions.
- One firm used an independent new-products group to run the process and track sign-offs (the tracking role an agent plus record could take).
