## Relevance to the project
GitLab's first-party account of how it keeps one company-wide written source of truth trusted: anyone may propose a change by merge request, but code owners and a directly responsible individual approve it, and chat history expires after 90 days so that decisions must land in the handbook. It bears on which trust mechanism to use: open contribution with owner approval (submitter and approver split), rather than owner-written only or fully open. It also names the failure the project fears: wikis fall out of date and people stop trusting them without checking with a human.

## Themes
- context-trust-mechanisms: "GitLab merge requests split the role of submitter and approver ... code owners and DRIs ... are ultimately responsible for reviewing each proposal and merging" (section "Empower the entire team to evolve the handbook").
- compounding-context-repo: "Document the solution first, then announce via Slack or email"; only 90 days of Slack is retained, as a forcing function to put work in the single source of truth (sections "Terminology" and "Let your Slack or chat messages expire quickly").

## Key points
- Why wikis lose trust, in GitLab's words: they are updated by a select few, so "content frequently falls out of date, which triggers a company-wide belief that the information cannot be trusted without personally confirming with another human" (section "Tools for building a handbook"). That is the trust-loss loop: staleness leads to verification by asking, which makes the store pointless.
- Provenance by construction: discussions start in issues or merge requests, "not in Slack", so whatever is merged "has a proper trail, full of context" (Slack retention section).
- Partial acceptance is normal: an example merge request where only part of a proposal was merged, so all parties had a voice (same section as the submitter/approver split).
- "Handbooks are never finished": iterative, process by process, rather than complete-before-launch (section "Documentation is never finished").
- No measured outcomes are given (no accuracy, freshness or usage figures); claims of "almost supernatural efficiency" are GitLab's own.
