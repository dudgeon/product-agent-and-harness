## Relevance to the project
PMI's annual survey, source of the "9.9% of every dollar wasted due to poor project performance" figure. Its failure-cause question is the most useful part for a dependency agent: shifting organisational priorities, changing objectives and inaccurate requirements top the list, while "resource dependency" and "task dependency" are cited by a minority. Labelled vendor-claim because PMI sells the certifications and practices whose value the survey argues for; figures are self-reported by practitioners.

## Themes
- program-failure-causes: primary causes of failed projects (select up to 3), in the report's chart order: change in organisation's priorities, change in project objectives, inaccurate requirements gathering, inadequate vision or goal, inadequate/poor communication, opportunities and risks not defined, inaccurate cost estimates, poor change management, inadequate sponsor support, resource dependency, inaccurate task time estimate, inexperienced project manager, limited/taxed resources, inadequate resource forecasting, team member procrastination, task dependency, other (appendix chart).
- process-governance-load: "9.9% of every dollar is wasted due to poor project performance", down from 13.5% in 2013; "champions" waste 1.4% versus 29.1% for "underperformers" (report body, Champions comparison).
- coordination-cost-and-waiting: 52% of projects completed in the past 12 months experienced scope creep, up from 43% five years earlier; projects of high complexity rose from 35% in 2013 to 41% (Control Scope section).

## Key points
- Sample: 4,455 project management practitioners, 447 senior executives and 800 PMO directors (5,402 surveyed), across government, IT, telecom, energy, manufacturing, healthcare and construction; online survey in October 2017; plus 16 interviews.
- Organisations with more than 80% of projects having executive sponsors report 40% more successful projects; 26% overall (41% of underperformers, 17% of champions) cite inadequate sponsor support as a primary cause of failure.
- Waste figures are respondents' estimates, not measured spend.
